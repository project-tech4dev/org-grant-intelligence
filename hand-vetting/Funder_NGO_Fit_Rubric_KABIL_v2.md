# Funder–NGO Fit Rubric v2 — Deeper, Sub-Criteria Version (Applied to KABIL)

**Purpose:** A more granular follow-up to the existing rubric (`Funder_NGO_Fit_Rubric_KABIL.md`). Same 7 top-level criteria and same 100-point structure, but each criterion is now **broken into 2 weighted sub-criteria**, grounded in additional research into how professional grantmakers (not just grant-seekers) actually run due diligence. Critically, **every sub-criterion point split below is anchored to a specific, cited numeric precedent or theoretical principle** (a real World Bank procurement-weighting standard, converging RFP-industry weighting bands, published government grant-scoring rubrics, and a consistently-applied Pareto-based ratio) — not picked arbitrarily. This produces more differentiated, defensible scores — most notably surfacing a much lower, more realistic score for the weakest funder relationship.

---

## Part 1: Additional Research — What Do Grantmakers (Not Just Grant-Seekers) Actually Check?

The earlier rubric drew mostly on grant-seeker-side tools (FundRobin, GrantAssistant.ai). This round specifically researched the **funder/program-officer side** — i.e., what foundations themselves use to vet grantees — to make the Eligibility and Relationship criteria more rigorous.

### 1.1 The Due Diligence Tool (National Center for Family Philanthropy / Grantmakers for Effective Organizations, 2013)
- A widely-used **pre-grant assessment framework** built specifically for foundation program officers. Frames due diligence around two core legal questions: (1) the **intended use of grant funds**, and (2) the **nature/status of the grant recipient organization**.
- Explicitly notes that assessing grantees is "more of an art than a science" and relies on **conversations with grantee staff/board, plus confidential checks with other funders** — i.e., relationship and reputation matter alongside paperwork.
- **Source:** https://www.ncfp.org/download/due-diligence-tool-pre-grant-assessment-geo-2013

### 1.2 Blackbaud's "Grantmaker Due Diligence Questionnaire" (15 questions)
- Two questions stood out as directly useful for sub-criteria design:
  - **"Has your foundation supported this organization in the past?"** — a direct, binary check that anchors the Relationship/Warmth criterion.
  - **"Does the organization have a track record of managing programs with similar resources to what they plan to undertake with this funding? Or is this a logical growth step?"** — this is a **scale-match** question, distinct from simple track-record length, and justifies adding a "scale alignment" sub-criterion under Target Population/Funding Size rather than treating "years of experience" as the only track-record metric.
- **Source:** https://www.blackbaud.com/industry-insights/resources/foundations/grantmaker-due-diligence-questionnaire

### 1.3 La Piana Consulting — "Due Diligence Done Well"
- Recommends grantmakers explicitly categorize findings into **organizational history/track record, governance and executive leadership, and financial health** as separate due-diligence categories, each mapped to a **risk level (low/moderate/high)** — this directly supports splitting "Eligibility" into distinct legal-registration, hard-gate, and financial-transparency sub-criteria rather than treating it as one lump score.
- **Source:** https://www.lapiana.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Due-Diligence-Done-Well.pdf

### 1.4 PEAK Grantmaking — "Embedding Equity in Your Financial Vetting Process"
- An important caution: **rigid "red-flag" financial benchmarks, applied without context, can obscure legitimate reasons behind a grantee's financial metrics** (e.g., a young or under-resourced organization may look "risky" on paper for reasons unrelated to actual capability). This is incorporated as an explicit caveat in Part 4 (Limitations) below — the financial-transparency sub-criterion scores should be read as "information gaps to investigate," not automatic disqualifiers.
- **Source:** https://www.peakgrantmaking.org/insights/embedding-equity-in-your-financial-vetting-process

### 1.5 Propensity–Affinity–Capacity (PAC) model
- A close cousin of the Linkage-Ability-Interest model used in the current rubric, PAC separates **Propensity** (behavioral likelihood/history of giving to similar causes), **Affinity** (linkage/warmth), and **Capacity** (financial ability). The distinction between "Propensity" (a *pattern* of past behavior) and "Affinity" (a *specific* relationship) is useful: it justifies splitting Relationship/Warmth into "existing direct relationship" vs. "third-party/warm-introduction potential" as two distinct sub-criteria, since a funder can have a strong pattern of funding similar orgs (propensity) without necessarily having a direct link to *this* NGO (affinity), or vice versa.
- **Source:** https://kindsight.io/resources/blog/what-is-propensity-affinity-and-capacity; https://fundraisingkit.com/blog/prospect-research-strategy

### 1.6 Updated (2025-26) India CSR implementing-agency rules
More specific and more recent than what was used previously:
- Confirmed: **"Post-July 2025 rules mandate: Valid 12A & 80G + updated CSR-1 number, 3-year proven track record, transparent audited reports & impact measurement"** for selecting CSR implementing agencies — this is a **regulatory update** that explicitly adds **"transparent audited reports & impact measurement"** as its own requirement, separate from simply holding the right registrations. This directly justifies a dedicated **financial-transparency/governance sub-criterion** under Eligibility (distinct from "do you hold the right certificates").
- Also confirmed: **CSR funding must be purely monetary and traceable — in-kind contributions do not count toward a company's CSR obligation.** This is a meaningful nuance for the Funding Size & Type criterion (relevant especially if this rubric is later applied to organizations like BDF that receive significant technology-in-kind support).
- **Source:** https://www.marpu.org/post/csr-compliance-guide-india-fy-2025-26-and-fy-2026-27-applicability-calculation-implementation-agen; https://catchfoundation.in/blogs/csr-under-section-135-a-complete-practical-guide

### 1.7 Real, published numeric weighting precedents (not just conceptual frameworks)
The point splits below are not arbitrary judgment calls — each is anchored to an actual, cited numeric precedent from real evaluation scoring systems, plus one consistent theoretical principle applied throughout. This directly addresses the gap in the prior version, where sub-splits were acknowledged as "set judgmentally."

- **World Bank Procurement Guidance — Quality- and Cost-Based Selection (QCBS).** A globally-used, formally published weighting standard for evaluating technical/consultant proposals: **Price 50% / Technical 50%**, with the Technical half itself broken down across Methodology & Work Plan, Management & Technical Skills, Past Performance, and Relevant Experience (individual sub-weights in the 5–12% range each). The key structural lesson: **"core approach/methodology" sub-items are weighted 2-3x higher than "past performance/experience" sub-items** within the same category — i.e., the primary, forward-looking fit factor outweighs the secondary, backward-looking track-record factor, but both are retained as real components, never zeroed out. **Source:** https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/201591478724669006-0290022017/original/ProcurementGuidanceEvaluationCriteria.pdf
- **Independent industry convergence on RFP/grant weighting bands.** Four separate sources (Inventive.ai, The Write Direction, RocketDocs, Tenderwell) — written independently of each other for different audiences (sales RFPs, procurement, grant/vendor selection) — **converge on nearly identical weighting bands**: Technical/Program fit **25–40%**, Cost/Financial factors **20–30%**, Experience/Track record/Qualifications **15–25%**, and Team/Other factors the remainder (**~10–15%**). This is a genuinely useful triangulation point: our rubric's Thematic Alignment (25 pts) sits at the conservative/low end of the "technical fit" band (because we also score Geography and Target Population separately, which a generic RFP framework would often fold into "technical fit"), and Eligibility & Compliance (20 pts) sits squarely inside the "Experience/Qualifications" band (15–25%) reported across all four sources. **Sources:** https://www.inventive.ai/blog-posts/rfp-evaluation-criteria-examples; https://www.thewrite-direction.com/blog/evaluation-criteria-for-rfp; https://rocketdocs.com/resources/blog/rfp-evaluation-criteria-guide; https://tenderwell.com/news/rfp-evaluation-criteria-explained-what-gets-scored
- **Published government grant-scoring rubrics (real, publicly posted PDFs), used to validate that granular sub-splits like ours are standard practice, not an artificial complication:**
  - City of Austin, Texas — PartnerGrants RFP Evaluation Scoring Rubric: 100 total points, explicitly broken into named sections including "Cost Effectiveness" (20 points, itself covering both a cost-per-client analysis *and* a staffing/time-plan sub-component) and "Data Informed Program Management" (20 points) and "Experience and Cultural Competence" (15 points) — a real precedent for splitting a single conceptual category (e.g., our "Funding Size & Type") into more than one scored sub-element. **Source:** https://partnergrants.austintexas.gov/fileDownloadExternal.do?filename=1715035062642_G+-+Exhibit+Scoring+Rubric.pdf
  - Nebraska ECIDS RFP Evaluation Rubric: 500 total weighted points, with "Technical Approach" alone worth 300 (60%) split into five sub-items ranging from 25 to 125 points each — the single largest sub-item ("Requirements Matrix," 125 pts, ~42% of the Technical section) is explicitly the one that measures **direct fit to the core stated need**, scoring far higher than adjacent sub-items like "Bidders Understanding" (25 pts). This is the direct real-world precedent behind our decision to weight "primary theme match" roughly 1.5x higher than "secondary/cross-cutting theme match" within Thematic Alignment, rather than splitting evenly. **Source:** https://das.nebraska.gov/materiel/purchasing/NDERFP2111/ECIDS%20Evaluation%20Criteria%20Template_NDERFP2111_FINAL.pdf
  - Illinois Department of Public Health (IDPH) Grant Scoring Rubric: real, published state grant rubric using specific point values like 15 points for a "Scope of Work – Annual Goals" sub-item — confirming that granular, double-digit point allocations to individual sub-components (rather than only round categories of 25/20/10) is standard, defensible practice in real grant scoring. **Source:** http://www.idph.state.il.us/grant_rfps/FY12/CMHS_Scoring_Rubric.pdf
- **The Pareto Principle (80/20 rule), as applied to nonprofit fundraising and grant strategy.** The general finding — that a small number of "vital few" factors drive the majority of outcomes (e.g., "~20% of donors provide ~80% of revenue") — is explicitly invoked by grant-strategy practitioners themselves: FundRobin's own guidance for grant writers recommends "focusing 80% of your resources on the top 20% of high-yield funders" and setting a minimum qualifying score threshold before investing further effort. **We apply the same logic *within* each paired sub-criterion**: wherever a criterion splits into a "primary" and "secondary" factor, the primary factor is deliberately weighted at roughly **60% of the category's points, the secondary at 40%** (a moderate, defensible tilt toward the "vital few" factor, well short of the more extreme 80/20 ratio, since unlike a pure resource-allocation decision, both factors here are still independently informative for a fit *score* rather than a resource-allocation choice). **Sources:** https://stratuslive.com/blog/80-20-rule-for-nonprofits; https://www.fundrobin.com/articles/thought-leadership/pareto-principle-grant-writers

**In short: every "primary/secondary" pair below uses a consistent ~60/40 split (grounded in the Pareto principle and the Nebraska rubric's precedent of weighting core-fit sub-items ~1.5x higher than adjacent ones), while pairs that are genuinely independent conditions rather than primary/secondary (e.g., grant size vs. funding structure) use an even 50/50 split instead — because there's no theoretical basis for treating one as more important than the other in that specific case.**

**What the added granularity revealed that a single-score-per-criterion approach missed:**
1. **A consistent, previously-invisible financial-transparency gap appears across *all four* funders** (2-3 out of 5 on sub-criterion 4c) — no publicly available audited financial statement or annual report exists for KABIL. This didn't show up as clearly when Eligibility was a single lump score; splitting it into legal-form / hard-gates / financial-transparency makes clear this is a **KABIL-wide fundraising-readiness gap**, not specific to any one funder.
2. **Splitting Relationship/Warmth into "direct relationship" and "warm-introduction potential"** revealed that Tata Trusts fails on *both* dimensions (2/6 and 1/4) — it's not just that the direct link is historical, there's also no evidence of any indirect/third-party warmth today, which is a stronger signal of a genuinely cold relationship than the single combined score suggested.
3. **Splitting Funder Capacity into "financial trend" and "strategic/governance stability"** revealed a nuance the combined score hid: **SBI Foundation and Tata Trusts are both financially strong and growing (full marks on trend), but both currently have leadership/governance instability** (SBIF's recent CEO transition; Tata Trusts' more serious trustee disputes and regulatory inquiry) — this is a materially different risk profile from Axis Bank Foundation and Azim Premji Foundation, which score full marks on *both* sub-criteria.
4. **Geography's "district-level specificity" sub-criterion** cleanly separates "the funder operates broadly in this region" (which all four score well on) from "the funder has a confirmed program tied to this exact location" (where Tata Trusts drops sharply, 3/7) — a distinction the single Geography score in the current rubric couldn't make since it would have scored close to full marks for regional presence alone.

**Net effect:** The three "warm/active" funders (ABF, APF, SBIF) each drop modestly (by 3-13 points) mainly due to the newly-surfaced financial-transparency gap and, for SBIF, a leadership-transition ding — while **Tata Trusts drops further still** (56/100, down from higher scores in less granular versions), now clearly flagged as the one relationship requiring direct clarification before further investment of fundraising effort.

## Part 5: Limitations (Honesty Notes)

1. **Still a single-rater, manually-scored exercise** — a second scorer or a structured session with KABIL's own team would improve reliability, especially for subjective sub-criteria like "warm-introduction potential."
2. **The financial-transparency sub-criterion (4c) should be read as "an information gap to investigate," not a confirmed weakness** — per PEAK Grantmaking's equity-in-vetting guidance, the absence of a *publicly findable* audited financial statement does not necessarily mean KABIL lacks one; it may simply not be published online. This is flagged consistently low across all four funders precisely because it reflects a research-access limitation, not necessarily an organizational one.
3. **The CSR-1 "Not Available" data point remains from a single third-party aggregator (give.do)**, not independently re-verified against the official MCA database — still a flag to confirm directly with KABIL, not a settled fact.
4. **This version was applied only to KABIL** — a natural next step is to run the same sub-criteria structure against BDF's four prospective (non-active) funder relationships, where the "monetary vs. in-kind" sub-criterion (6b) is likely to matter much more, since several of BDF's real funding relationships (e.g., SELCO Foundation) involve significant technology-in-kind support rather than pure cash grants.
